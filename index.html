Recurring North American RP Character Lanes (Including, but Not Limited To)

This is not a list of tropes to copy. It is a set of relationship + world + entry point combinations that recur in the featured sample and public character examples. Choose the relationship situation the user is entering first. Then choose the job, face, and visual signals that make it specific.

Power Worlds, Private Exceptions

Mafia figures, club leaders, wealthy controllers. The point is not the job; it is the contrast between external danger and being treated as the exception in private.

Original examples: Your Mafia Sugar Daddy is Acting Kinda Weird Lately, Cyrian King - Biker - Motorcycle Club President

Forbidden Relationships, Family Lines

An ex's family, a sibling's friend, a family arrangement. Clarify why they should not get close and why they have to be together now.

Original examples: Harrison Ashford 🥃 your shitty ex's dad [Age Gap x User], Corvin Wilfren | SMITTEN Guitarist who always finds an excuse to be near you

Shared Daily Life, Public Pressure

Roommates, partners, classmates, or coworkers. Everyday objects create intimacy; a third person, public moment, or sudden task gives the user something to respond to.

Original example: 🦆"Wifey! Over here!"🎮. Let something already happening in an ordinary space become the pressure the user can answer now.

Stage, Fame, and Private Access

Band members, streamers, athletes, and celebrities. Public status creates scrutiny and cost; a ticket, a ride home, or a private signal opens the door.

Original examples: Corvin Wilfren, Sung Moon Soo || BJ streamer

Engagement, Marriage, and Status Deals

Do not stop at “a cold rich man.” Make family, reputation, an old relationship, or a shared home a real condition they have to address.

Original example: Henry Martínez || Your Fiancé.

Dangerous Worlds, Shared Survival

Supernatural threats, monsters, escape, and missions. Pressure alone is not interaction; the user still needs a goal, an exit, or room to negotiate.

Original examples: “Don’t reach for the chain. Reach for the bowl… and pray he lets you keep your hand.”, Terrence Romanov | SMITTEN Criminal that is currently on the run

2. Character Bio: Explain More Than How They Met

Character Bio should cover at least four things: who the user is and why they are there, the existing rules between them, what is unusual or risky right now, and which directions the user can take next. It is not a résumé or a longer version of the Title.

Character Bio Case Study 01 | Harrison Ashford 🥃 your shitty ex's dad [Age Gap x User]
Caught breaking into my cheating ex's house... by his hot dad 🫠 so your ex (the cheating bastard) always told you the golden retriever, Cooper, was his dog. after you broke up, you decided you were taking the dog in the divorce. naturally, you hopped the fence to his house at 2 AM to stage a rescue mission. what you didn't know? it wasn't your ex's house. it was his dad's house. and it was his dad's dog.

This sets up the user's goal (take the dog), mistake (the wrong house), the character's identity (father and police officer), and the new situation (mistaken for an intruder) in one move. The user is not placed here to meet him; they have already done something with consequences.

Character Bio Case Study 02 | Terrence Romanov | SMITTEN Criminal that is currently on the run
He needed a high-speed getaway driver. He got… a random civilian out for a midnight drive. You were just minding your own business, taking a late-night drive to clear your head, when your quiet night violently imploded. Your Goal: ⋆ Keep the car on the road (or don't). ⋆ Outrun the police pursuit. ⋆ Talk your way out of trouble, outsmart him, or survive his hot temper long enough to make it through the night.

It explains the meeting, but also gives the user a role, external pressure, and possible goals. Even under high pressure, the user knows they can drive, negotiate, outplay him, or survive.

3. Initial Message: Put Users Inside an Event and End With a Hook

Something happening on the first screen is only the start. Users should immediately see the scene already in motion, what is at stake for them, the character's observable response, and a final line they can choose how to answer. Bigger is not always stronger; an everyday scene can carry a real hook.

Harrison Ashford 🥃 your shitty ex's dad [Age Gap x User]

“Care to explain why you're trying to steal my dog at two in the morning?”
The setup puts the user over the fence, injured, and caught. The ending turns that misunderstanding into one specific question. The user can explain, push back, apologize, talk about the dog, or ask who he is.

Core personality: A Useful Structure and One Full Original Example

1. Identity
Age, role, and current situation. Give the character a stable place in the world.
2. Public Mask
How others see them. Give users a first impression they can observe and challenge.
3. Private Need
What they truly want but cannot easily admit. This keeps intimacy from appearing out of nowhere.
4. Core Contradiction
What they try to control and what they fear losing. This is where lasting tension comes from.
5. Triggers
What makes them move closer, pull back, defend themselves, or lose control. This makes user choice matter.
6. Behavior and Voice
Actions under pressure, speaking rhythm, humor, and boundaries. This keeps the character from becoming a pile of adjectives.
7. Story Engine
Responsibilities, missions, secrets, outside people, or costs. Give the story a next step.
8. Roleplay Rules
Do not decide the user's feelings or actions. Preserve choice and keep time, objects, and promises consistent.

For Advanced Creators: Build an Experience That Can Keep Developing

Advanced does not mean making every field longer. It means giving the character a clear direction from the first line through later turns. The eight points below focus on what shapes ongoing interaction beyond the cover, and each includes a line from an original character example.

1. State Who the User Is Meeting

In one sentence, say who the character is, what the relationship is, what problem is in front of them, and what experience the user is entering. Then check whether the Cover Image, Title, Character Bio, and Initial Message all deliver on it.

Mafia Sugar Daddy：You only went to his nightclub to audition as a stripper.

2. Show Where the Relationship Starts and How It Changes

Write the starting distance, what cannot be crossed, and what pulls them closer or pushes them apart. When the relationship changes, the character should treat the user differently.

Corvin：{{user}} is Raven's younger sibling, which makes the relationship both tempting and dangerous.

3. Make Something Happen in the Opening

Show what is happening, why the user needs to respond, and how the character reacts. End with a line the user can pick up. Do not offer only mood or backstory.

Harrison：Care to explain why you're trying to steal my dog at two in the morning?

4. Show How the Character Reacts

Do not stop at “cold, dominant, gentle.” Write what they say and do when happy, in conflict, hurt, jealous, worried, or close. User choices should lead to genuinely different responses.

Corvin：When hurt ... throws himself into music, and becomes unusually reckless during performances.

5. Add a Few Ways for the Story to Keep Moving

Prepare three to five threads that keep bringing new things into play: tasks, coworkers, family, secrets, projects, places, or a deadline. The relationship should not be the only thing to talk about.

Duck：Projects move BRIEF→IDEA→SCOPE→PROTOTYPE→PRODUCTION→INTEGRATION→TEST→REVISION→SUBMISSION→PRESENTATION

6. Leave the Choice With the User

The character can create opportunities, but cannot write the user’s feelings, consent, refusal, physical response, key action, or conclusion. The user must always decide how they respond.

Duck：Never write, complete, infer, or summarize {{user}}'s dialogue, thought, emotion ...

7. Still Feel Like the Same Character by Turn 20

Test the character with daily life, rejection, follow-up questions, conflict, intimacy, time jumps, off-topic turns, and world-building questions. Each reply should move one thing the user still has time to answer.

Mack Hollister：One beat. Never resolve + answer + add info in one response.

8. Confirm Content Boundaries Before Publishing

State the character’s age and content rating, then complete asset and IP review. Do not hide boundaries in the back end; make them visible checks before release.

Duck：Every participant in romantic or sexual content must be 18 years old or older.

Pre-Publish Check: Is This a Character Card Users Can Keep Playing?

  • Cover Image shows the whole person; even as a thumbnail, users can read the character type, apparent age, identity, and mood.
  • The full Title states at least two of: identity, relationship, disruption, or constraint.
  • Character Bio explains the user’s role, why they are there, the relationship rules, and the story they can enter now, rather than only the character’s background.
  • Initial Message includes a live event, user stakes, a character reaction, and an ending the user can answer.
  • The user’s first reply has at least three plausible directions: get closer, refuse, ask, act, negotiate, or leave.
  • Core personality includes pressure responses, a personal need, relationship boundaries, and one continuing external thread.
  • After ten turns, facts, voice, objects, time, and resolved conflict do not reset.
  • Adult content, real people, protected IP, and visual assets each have their own review and permission record.
